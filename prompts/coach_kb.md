# Conditional coaching knowledge base

Use an entry as a question to check against the film, not as a diagnosis. The wording below is
an editorial application for ordinary amateur players. Source pointers support broad principles;
they do not prove the mechanism in a particular game or validate our chosen practice format.

- Start with information visible before the action. Separate choice, execution and outcome.
- Recurring claims need independent possessions. A useful single example stays `single_episode`.
- Check the exception and a counterexample. Several reasonable choices or an unclear judgement are valid.
- Do not infer tiredness, confidence, intent, team tactics or medical causes from an outcome.
- Track real opportunities, suitable choices, execution and results separately; missing counts stay unknown.
- Practice descriptions are adjustable experiments inside the existing time budget, not additional workload.
  Any numeric success target needs its own explicit basis; these entries set no universal quota.
- Source page numbers below are inherited library-copy references. Verify the actual edition and passage
  before making a precise source claim. Current official manuals: https://about.fiba.basketball/en/wabc-documents.
  Keep source-backed principles, editorial conditions and the player’s own observations distinct.

### KB-transition-defence

**Symptom (observable):** Possession changes while an opponent runs toward the basket. Locate the ball, deepest runner and available defenders before an attempted interception.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** Leaving the deepest runner without cover can let a pass bypass the defender challenging the ball. A scoring outcome alone does not establish this sequence.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** When you are the last available defender, stay between the threat and the basket. Challenge a pass when it is reachable and the runners behind you are covered.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** A teammate may already protect the basket, or the ball may be safely interceptable. A visible retreating defender disproves “nobody got back.” Do not infer fatigue, effort or an agreed role from running speed.

**Drill (training experiment):** Start a small transition game with different runner positions and a returning teammate. Vary whether the ball can be reached. Ask who covers the runner before replaying. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level1 pp. 270-273 (defence starts with the shot, safety to the top of the key, "basket before ball", jam the outlet without reaching, 1v2 and 2v3 tandem); jrnba-mvp-plans p. 25 (Line Up Transition Drill); jrnba-allstar-plans pp. 28-29 (3 on 2 Trailer Game). ### KB-press-break

**Symptom (observable):** Before a second defender arrives, identify reachable receivers, passing lanes and room to retreat.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** A trap can remove the dribble and passing angles; teammates who move into visible gaps may restore an option.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** When a trap approaches, use an available receiver or retreat into space while keeping control. Against individual pressure, spacing may give the handler room, while a teammate remains available if needed.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** An apparently open teammate may be screened by a defender or too far away for a safe pass. Clearing space does not guarantee that help cannot arrive. If receiver identity or lane is unclear, do not prescribe one mandatory pass.

**Drill (training experiment):** Use a small backcourt game where defenders vary between single pressure and a second defender. Receivers change position; review control and usable outlets, not only whether half court was crossed. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level1 pp. 365-368 (zone or trap press: retreat dribble at an angle, catch no closer to the baseline than the free-throw line, receivers in gaps, three passing options, pass over half way), p. 371 (5-area framework for practising against pressure), pp. 372-373 (man-to-man press: clear the court so the dribbler plays 1-on-1 with no trap, or let a different player bring it up; 8 seconds to reach the front court); fiba-wabc-level2 p. 102 (best pass against a double team is to the player the second defender left); jrnba-allstar-plans p. 34 (Half-Court Line Battles); https://www.coachesclipboard.net/BasketballPressOffenses.html. ### KB-half-court-spacing

**Symptom (observable):** At the start of a drive, locate the driving lane, nearby offensive players and their defenders.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** Two teammates occupying the same route may allow one defender to affect both. Spacing is useful when it creates a reachable passing option or room to attack.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** When a teammate drives toward your area, move to a reachable open spot or hold useful width, according to the team action.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** Standing can preserve an open passing target; movement can crowd a drive. A close grouping may be a screen or another intentional action. Low measured spacing alone is not a mistake.

**Drill (training experiment):** Play a small half-court game with defenders alternately helping and staying home. Pause at the drive and ask whether the receiver can be seen and reached. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level1 pp. 310-311 (basic floor spacing, head-hands-feet spots, one player per spot, cuts go into the key and come out), p. 246 (why young players crowd the ball: short passing range, eyes down when dribbling); fiba-wabc-level3 p. 202 (lifted middle pick-and-roll: three players on the perimeter create space to attack the rim); jrnba-allstar-plans p. 5 (2 on 1 Bunch Spacing Game).

### KB-off-ball-cuts

**Symptom (observable):** After passing, check the defender, the lane to the basket and the ball-handler’s next movement.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** A cut behind a defender watching the ball may create a target. A cut into an occupied lane may instead remove space.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** Cut when the route is open and the receiver can find you; otherwise hold width, relocate or screen when it helps the current action.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** There is no every-pass cut rule. A teammate already driving, a crowded basket or a defender protecting the cut can make staying available the better choice. Useful stationary spacing is a counterexample to “standing means inactive.”

**Drill (training experiment):** In a small passing game, alternate denial, ball watching and help defence. Let the passer choose a cut, relocation or holding space; explain the choice. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level1 pp. 311-312 (v-cut, back cut when the defender is in the passing lane, pass and cut / give and go), p. 315 (replacing the cutter keeps the floor spaced), pp. 256-257 (shell drill stages: 5 ball reversals, must reverse twice and penetrate before shooting), p. 319 (ball reversal makes defenders move and opens drives), p. 325 (pass and screen away); jrnba-mvp-plans pp. 24-25 (4 on 4 Shell to Rebound).

### KB-drive-help

**Symptom (observable):** As the drive begins, see whether the first defender can still remain in front and identify nearby uncovered receivers.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** Help may stop a drive but expose another player. Once the ball is contained, remaining too close to it can leave a simple pass outside.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** Help when the driver is getting past the first defender and you can arrive safely. When a teammate contains the drive, find the uncovered receiver near you and move toward that player.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** Do not leave the basket if the driver remains uncontained. Existing team coverage, receiver skill and distance change the choice. A defender staying in front and containing the drive can contradict an assumed need for another helper; position alone does not prove containment.

**Drill (training experiment):** Use three attackers and defenders; vary whether the first defender is beaten. The next defender chooses help or recovery while seeing both ball and receiver. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level1 pp. 260-261 (help and recover, step with both feet, do not reach), pp. 263-265 (helping the helper: secondary rotation to protect the basket, the top defender covers two players), p. 267 ("Basket-High-Ball" rotation activity), p. 268 (Disadvantage Shell Drill); fiba-wabc-level3 pp. 83-85 (pack line defence: protect against dribble penetration, help position halfway between man and ball; weaknesses: constant closeouts, good perimeter shooters, not before players can close out and contain); jrnba-mvp-plans pp. 12-13 (3 on 3 From Help the Helper).

### KB-closeouts

**Symptom (observable):** When a pass travels to a receiver, check distance, the receiver’s preparation and the route behind the closing defender.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** A controlled approach can contest a shot while retaining a chance to follow a drive. Running past may surrender the route to the basket.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** Move as the pass travels, then adjust speed and distance so you can contest without losing balance. Use the team’s help position when choosing the approach.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** A prepared shooter and a driver may require different distances. A very late defender may not be able to contest safely. A made contested shot does not prove a poor closeout.

**Drill (training experiment):** The receiver randomly shoots, drives or passes. Change the starting distance and available help; review balance and the next defensive action. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level1 pp. 140-142 (closing out: fly with the ball, sprint, chop steps, two hands high, call "ball", practise contested; close-out activities 1-4); fiba-wabc-level2 p. 90 (close out short against a driver), pp. 91-92 (closing out a shooter: run past the shooting shoulder without fouling; Close Out Shooter activity); fiba-wabc-level3 pp. 194-195 (corner kick-outs force the longest closeouts); jrnba-allstar-plans p. 41 (2 Person Close-Out to Live Game).

### KB-defensive-rebounding

**Symptom (observable):** When the shot goes up, locate a nearby opponent, the ball’s path and the space needed to secure it.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** Watching only the ball can allow a nearby attacker to move through the rebound space. Winning initial position still does not guarantee the bounce.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** Find the nearby rebound threat, establish legal position and pursue the ball. Continue until the team controls it before treating the possession as finished.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** A long or unusual bounce, distant attacker or teammate already securing the ball changes the job. A lost rebound alone does not identify who failed to block out.

**Drill (training experiment):** Vary shot direction and rebounder starting positions in a small live game. Keep the play live until control; record initial position and control separately. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level1 pp. 156-157 (blocking out: see, balance, contact, turn, hands up; one step away from the basket; help defenders step to the edge of the key), p. 160 ("Split Line Box Out" and "Penetrate & Rebound" activities); jrnba-mvp-plans pp. 24-25 (4 on 4 Shell to Rebound).

### KB-offensive-rebounding-balance

**Symptom (observable):** Before the shot, identify who can contest the rebound and who can cover opponents moving toward the other basket.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** Chasing a rebound can create a second chance but leave fewer defenders behind the ball. Team balance depends on positions and responsibilities.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** Use an agreed balance suited to the players and the shot: nearby players may pursue the rebound while others cover the retreat. Recheck when the ball changes hands.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** No universal two-one-two split is required. Shot location, team roles and opponents already running change the choice. Do not assign fault without knowing the role or seeing coverage.

**Drill (training experiment):** Play through misses with varied shot locations and opponents who sometimes run early. Review rebound chances and protection behind the ball together. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level1 pp. 375-376 (rebounding and defensive transition: anticipate the shot, assign rebounding pathways, reward moving to a rebounding spot, play activities on to a score or defensive control), p. 270 (on a shot, each player goes either into the key to rebound or to the top of the key to defend the break). The 2-1-2 split and the "half" rebounder were a previous editorial example, not a current universal rule or a requirement in these pages.

### KB-foul-discipline

**Symptom (observable):** Before contact, identify the defender’s position, direction and reachable ball; distinguish a whistle from an inferred foul cause.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** Reaching after losing position may contact the attacker without restoring containment. Legal body position and controlled hands can limit that risk.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** Try to regain a position between attacker and basket, contest within the applicable rules, and avoid a late reach when the ball is not available.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** A reachable ball can be challenged legally; not every attempt from the side is a foul. An obscured contact or uncertain whistle remains unclear. Check current competition rules before explaining penalties.

**Drill (training experiment):** Use live drives with a defender starting in different positions. Review position and contact before the outcome; avoid rewarding unsafe contact just because it stops a score. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level3 p. 151 (late-clock fouls from reaching; the "no grab" practice rule), p. 155 (after 4 team fouls in a quarter every foul gives two free throws; fouling the last man on a break without playing the ball is unsportsmanlike); fiba-wabc-level2 p. 95 (do not reach beyond your cylinder or hit the shooter before he lands), p. 102 (hands high and active, not reaching for the ball); fiba-wabc-level1 p. 272 (resist reaching on the outlet, it often results in a foul); FIBA Official Basketball Rules, Art. 33 (cylinder, verticality, legal guarding position), https://about.fiba.basketball/en/our-sport/official-basketball-rules; Dean Oliver's four factors (free throws factor FT/FGA), https://www.basketball-reference.com/about/factors.html.

### KB-shot-selection

**Symptom (observable):** Before release, check balance, familiar range, defender distance, available teammates and time remaining.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** Preparation and space can allow a controlled attempt. A pass can use a better teammate opportunity; neither action must produce a score to be reasonable.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** Shoot when prepared in a suitable range with a feasible window. If a teammate can continue attacking from a better position and the pass is available, pass directly.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** There is no mandatory paint touch or shot quota. Player skill, remaining time and defensive movement can make several choices reasonable. A make does not rescue a poor choice; a miss does not invalidate a good one.

**Drill (training experiment):** A defender varies distance and approach while a second receiver is sometimes better placed. The attacker can shoot, drive or pass; score decision and execution separately from the result. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level3 pp. 193-195 (shot value of twos vs threes, corner three created by penetration and kick-outs, mostly assisted); fiba-wabc-level1 p. 257 (shell stage 2: penetrate the key before shooting), pp. 321-323 (dribble penetration: both feet in the key, jump stop, be a scorer first; receiver spots, the player opposite flares for the shot), p. 375 (rebounders present decide whether a shot is a good option); jrnba-allstar-plans pp. 25-26 (Free Throw Golf); https://www.coachesclipboard.net/basketball-shot-selection.html; Dean Oliver's four factors, shooting weighs most, https://www.basketball-reference.com/about/factors.html.

### KB-pick-and-roll

**Symptom (observable):** At a ball screen, locate both immediate defenders, the screener’s route and deeper help.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** The screen changes which defender can reach each attacker. A coordinated response can cover ball and screener; a mismatch between responsibilities may expose one.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** On defence use the team’s agreed coverage and identify who takes the screener. On offence read the immediate defenders and deeper help before choosing the available shot, drive or pass.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** No single coverage fits every team or opponent. Do not impose a hard show or a fixed number of dribbles. A roll can be covered from deeper in the lane even when the first pass appears open.

**Drill (training experiment):** Play a small game starting with a screen. Change the coverage and deeper helper; ask players to identify who was available before judging the outcome. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level3 pp. 96-100 (defending on-ball screens: decisive, coordinated, the team acting in unison matters more than the tactic; screen defender steps vertically; over vs under by shooter; weak side sags into the key; p. 100 the screener's defender shows strongly and recovers), pp. 201-202 (pick and roll offence: screener sets up, sprints into the screen, angle, then roll or pop; other players replace and space); fiba-wabc-level1 p. 298 (screener's defender steps out to "show their number", then recovers); jrnba-mvp-plans p. 43 (3 on 3 Dictated Pick & Roll; calling the coverage aloud is our addition).

### KB-late-clock-end-of-period

**Symptom (observable):** Establish the actual score, possession and reliable remaining time before evaluating a late decision.

**Why it happens (hypothesis to verify):** The time available changes which actions can finish; a longer setup may remove a later option. A result alone does not show whether the clock was known.

**What to check on film:** Reopen the sequence before and after the choice. Identify the relevant people, timing and feasible options; record any hidden view or identity uncertainty.

**In-game rule (conditional):** Choose an action the group can complete within the available time. Communicate time and responsibility; on defence avoid unnecessary contact while retaining coverage.

**Exception and disconfirming evidence:** Time may be obscured or the video clock may differ from the game clock. Whether to hold, attack, take a two or seek a three depends on the actual situation; do not hard-code a universal release time.

**Drill (training experiment):** Use short live situations with different scores, possession and remaining times. State the situation first, then explain a feasible action and alternative. A solo version can rehearse the movement or verbal recognition, but does not test response to a live opponent.

**Next-game target (observation):** Review comparable opportunities using the cue above. Record the actual cue, chosen action, execution and result separately. Retain, revise or retire the reminder from later evidence; do not turn a selected clip count into a whole-game rate.

**Source:** fiba-wabc-level3 p. 151 (late shot clock defence: team rules, the clock as a defender, no-grab rule), pp. 234-235 (late shot clock offence: clock awareness drills, structures, the ball to the best decision-maker; a ball screen can backfire with an inexperienced team), p. 236 (last possession: run the clock to 5-7 s before shooting to leave time for a rebound; 2- and 3-point options; not fouling when up 3), p. 155 (the opposite option: fouling when up 3); jrnba-allstar-plans p. 29 (5 on 5 Situational).
